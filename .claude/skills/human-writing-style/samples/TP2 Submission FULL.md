**C. Case Summary**

In *Parker-Smith v. STO Corp.*, the homeowner, Helen Parker-Smith, sued STO Corp for product failure, which caused severe water damage and termite infestation in her home after just a few years. Helen sued for breach of the implied warranty of fitness, arguing that the manufacturer broke the implied warranty, as the product was supposed to protect the exterior of a house. Despite the product's failure, the court ruled in favor of the manufacturer because their disclaimer, included in written warranty documents, explicitly disclaimed the implied warranty of fitness.

The court's decision hinged on VA Code, 8.2-316, which allows sellers to disclaim implied warranties if the disclaimer is both in writing and conspicuous. "Conspicuous" implies that the disclaimers must be written in a way that a reasonable person would notice them and not hide them in tiny print. Given that the disclaimer was both written and conspicuous, the implied warranty never existed, meaning the manufacturer could not be held liable. Therefore, the manufacturer won the case.

**D. Legal Elements Affected**

This case directly helps defeat Legal Element 2: An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose existed. It will be used as a precedent to strengthen our argument against sub-element 2c, which requires Allmart's reliance on Fray to be reasonable.

**E. Explanation of How the Case Helps Our Defense**

This case is our strongest piece of evidence so far. It will be used to argue that an implied warranty never existed between Ware Fray and Allmart. This can be achieved by using the court's ruling in the *Helen* case, where the legal principle established was that a seller can proactively prevent an implied warranty from being created through the use of a clear disclaimer.

Just as the court found that STO Corp.'s written disclaimer was sufficient to eliminate the implied warranty, we will argue that Fray's multiple warnings will accomplish the same goal. His combination of notices—email stating "I have not fully tested my software," the software provided "as is," and the public list of known bugs—will collectively serve as a conspicuous warning to Allmart. These made it clear that there was no warranty and that Allmart was using the software at its own risk. **C. Case Summary**

Micro Products, Inc (Micro) filed a motion against Caliber Learning Network, Inc., and Sylvan Learning Centers, Inc (Caliber) for not paying the full amount for products Caliber purchased. In response, Caliber denied this and filed a counterclaim asserting that the products purchased from Micro were faulty and breached a warranty for fitness.

Caliber bought a system from Micro that would allow simultaneous training in different locations. Though they bought this system from Micro, they made adjustments to it and required new software to use. This software was developed but outsourced by Micro. Furthermore, Micro stated that the system should be used with high definition monitors, and Caliber adjusted it to use for televisions instead. These changes were still made, and a representative from Micro was aware of the faults these changes would cause and saw the connectivity issue during testing, but still oversaw the installation of the system while making no disclaimer.

After the faulty system was installed, fixes were attempted but were ultimately unsuccessful. This led to Caliber rejecting the acceptance of the system and refusing to pay the full amount, which initiated the case.

Caliber won the case due to breach of warranty of fitness for a particular purpose and was awarded money back. They won because the representative was aware of the potential issues, provided no warning of them, and still oversaw the installation of the system.

**C. Case Summary**

In *King Industries v. Worlco*, the plaintiff, King Industries, sued Worlco and a leasing company after a software system they purchased failed to perform as expected. King argued that Worlco recommended the system and that it should be held responsible under an implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose. However, the contract and lease documents both included clear disclaimers, stating the software was provided “as is” and that there were no warranties. These disclaimers were placed noticeably in the contract, in capital letters and near the signature line.

The court ruled in favor of Worlco. It held that the disclaimers met the requirements of Virginia Code § 8.2‑316 because they were in writing and conspicuous. As a result, the court found that no implied warranty ever existed. The court also ruled against the fraud claim, saying King had a duty to read the contracts and that any misunderstanding was their own responsibility.

**D. Legal Elements Affected**

* Legal Element 1: Fray was a “seller.”  
* Legal Element 2: An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose existed.

**E. Explanation of How the Case Helps Our Defense**  
This case is a strong legal precedent showing that a clear and visible disclaimer can prevent an implied warranty from forming, even when the buyer says they relied on the seller’s recommendations. We will argue that Fray’s disclaimer (that FreeMax was “beta,” “as is,” and untested) serves the same legal purpose.

Just as the court found Worlco’s written contract terms sufficient to block liability, we will argue that Fray’s multiple notices to Allmart acted as a conspicuous disclaimer. Fray stated that he did not guarantee FreeMax for any specific use, that the software was not fully tested, and that all known bugs were posted publicly. He also was not involved in Allmart’s testing or integration. This matches the reasoning in King, where even a real seller avoided liability by making the terms clear.

In fact, Fray is in an even stronger position; he never sold or licensed the software and was not paid. If a real seller like Worlco could legally disclaim responsibility, then a student offering free software should not be held to a higher standard. By analyzing the three cases provided, our understanding and defense of legal elements 1, 2, and 3 are further supplemented.

**Legal Element 1: The Defendant, Ware Fray, was a “seller” of goods.**  
In all three cases, a monetary transaction occurred and was the reason for the cases. The plaintiffs all sought money they believed they were owed. In regard to Fray, he was offering a free, open-source software and did not charge Allmart anything to use it. This makes Fray less responsible than those who provided monetary compensation in the transaction. Fray would not be held to the same standard as those who took money in exchange for the job/goods.

While Allmart might argue that the scholarship acts as a form of payment, we can counter that it was an independent award and doesn’t have a direct connection with the software. It will be important to distinguish it from the defendants in *King Industries* and *Parker Smith*, as they engaged in for-profit sales. We will argue that the “Uniform Commercial Code” was never triggered because there was no sale in the case of Fray’s academic project, unlike the commercial transactions in the precedent cases.

**Legal Element 2: An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose existed.**  
The analysis of *Helen Parker-Smith v. STO Corp.* and *King Industries, Inc. v. Worlco Data Systems, Inc.* shows the importance of a warranty and presents our case’s primary strength.

*Helen Parker-Smith v. STO Corp.* demonstrates the importance and power of a visible, written disclaimer of warranty. If Rudy Kipling was able to see and read the “as-is” disclaimer in the program, it is hard to argue that it wasn’t conspicuous and easily found. This is in addition to the extra warnings that Fray gave to Allmart. *King Industries, Inc. v. Worlco Data Systems, Inc.* further reinforces this idea, establishing that it is the responsibility of Allmart to ensure the software program is safe to be used since the disclaimer was made. Specifically, both precedent cases confirmed that under Virginia law, a clear and conspicuous disclaimer is sufficient to prevent an implied warranty from ever being created. We will use Fray’s multiple warnings to serve as the conspicuous disclaimer that Allmart should have respected.

**Legal Element 3: The implied warranty of fitness was breached.**  
With the analysis of *Micro Prods. v. Sylvan Learning Sys.*, it shows that breaching the implied warranty of fitness takes a lot. In that case, the system purchased from Micro by Caliber was working less than fifty percent of the time. If Fray’s program were to be working the majority of the time, it would be hard to establish this legal element to find Fray guilty.

Furthermore, the case shows all the additional things that need to happen for there to be a breach of implied warranty. Such as how a Micro representative had knowledge of the issues of the system prior to installation, made no warning/disclaimer of it, and still installed the system. This sequence of actions provides the implied warranty of fitness, as the representative allowed the transaction to continue. Fray knew there could be issues that he hadn’t spotted yet, and chose to make it known that this is a very possible situation, unlike the Micro representative.

Even if the legal element of the implied warranty of fitness was established, which would already be hard to do given the disclaimers Fray has made to Allmart and has written in the software program, this case analysis shows that Fray has to be aware of issues, not disclaim them, and still allow Allmart to use his program to breach the warranty. In addition, the *Micro Prods* case shows how the buyer’s reliance must be reasonable. With that, Allmart deploying the beta software in a mission-critical system despite multiple warnings can be deemed an unreasonable act. Therefore, there is no breach as the true cause of Allmart’s damages was not due to a minor bug in the software, but rather their own negligence in deploying experimental software without proper testing.
