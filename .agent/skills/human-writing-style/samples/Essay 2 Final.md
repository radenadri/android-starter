In today's world, AI can definitely be seen as an evolution that significantly accelerates human progress in just a few years that would've taken us decades to accomplish. It's undeniable that AI has definitely become part of our day-to-day lives. From using devices like Amazon Alexa to using generative AI to assist with finance decisions and writing plans, these tools are everywhere. With this, it's quite common to find similar scenarios even in the workplace. However, while certain aspects of AI use are acceptable, not all applications are appropriate.

Looking at the case scenario presented, I want to address some of the assumptions. The claim that "after feeding the specifications into the AI tool, you are quickly given a complete solution that integrates perfectly into the existing project" is actually not quite accurate within current technology. The fact is that these models are prone to hallucinations. In many iterations, they've shown that the model would go as far as making up false solutions or fabricating ways to pass unit test cases. Further, leaving the model to run unsupervised can increase the risk of creating technical debt and having to redo the work for various reasons.

Given realistic experience, the human engineer would actually be able to use AI as a foundation to construct solutions and guide the models to meet the requirements. Even assuming the specifications were perfect (which they often aren't unless you're building a pet project), the human engineer would have insights into the system they've built and could give further recommendations to optimize it, things like security patches, code splitting, and scalability considerations. If the model were perfect and could handle all of this alone, I strongly believe the company should get rid of the new developer, but as a contradiction, this "perfect model" could then do the senior engineer's job, along with the product manager's, the CEO's, and everyone else's responsibilities. At that point, we're facing a much larger issue. However, if an AI tool can produce a perfect solution from specs alone, this would definitely raise ethical and professional questions.

In this case, to analyze the developer's actions, Virtue Ethics will be used to determine whether the developer is moral and ethical based on the character of the person. As a virtuous person, the developer would be committed to learning and mastering their craft, meaning that they should use the tool as a supplement to their skill, instead of using it to delegate their core expertise. However, the developer's actions in this scenario are an issue as the person immediately offloads the entire task to a tool, then submits the work as their own without any disclaimers or clarification, which can be considered deception. This directly violates the Virtue Ethics of mastering their craft; the actual deterioration of the developer's skills shows a failure to be a good developer/engineer.

In addition to Virtue Ethics, we can also use Rule Utilitarianism to analyze this case. Unlike Act Utilitarianism, which asks for what rules that are followed by everyone would produce the greatest societal good. If we were to establish a rule stating, "Engineers may blindly submit AI-generated code without review to save time," the outcome would likely be catastrophic for most industries. While it might save time in the short term for one person, if every engineer did this, software reliability, trust in technology and critical infrastructure would all fail. Therefore, under Rule Utilitarianism, the most ethical course of action is to follow a rule such as, "Engineers must verify and understand all code they submit." This ensures stability, safety, and the continued functionality of the systems we rely on, ultimately maximizing happiness and minimizing harm for the greatest number of people.

One might argue against this by saying that the real job of an engineer in this scenario isn't just coding. Instead, the real responsibilities consist of requirements analysis, creating solutions, and validating the output that fits the business need. The AI is just there to translate the specs; it doesn't understand the context, reasoning, and why it should do something. However, this defense contains flaws and goes against both the Virtue Ethics belief and the Utilitarian perspective. As the developer's skills become rusty from the lack of coding, their ability to perform code review is also degraded, which is one of the most critical tasks.

Senior engineers are expected to catch issues that junior developers and automated tools might miss,but this ability comes from hands-on coding experience. A developer who solely relies on AI rather than putting in the work themselves won't be able to spot problematic patterns, as GenAI models are known to produce brute-force solutions that seem to work on the surface but often contain subtle but critical flaws. This then leads to a cascading effect where the reviewers who lack the experience would allow bad code to get approved and integrated into production systems. This problem is further compounded by a related issue; if AI can perform well on low-level development, all entry-level positions would no longer be needed. This creates a larger issue, as it destroys the very pipeline that trains developers to become the next senior or expert who are critical for validating the AI's work.

Regarding my experience with AI at GMU, I've definitely been using these tools to assist me to learn faster when I'm stuck on a concept. I'll ask AI to explain it from different angles until something clicks. Between the given scenario and my experience, the main difference is that I'm using it to understand, not to bypass understanding. When simply using AI to just generate code without thinking through the logic, properties of the syntax or the language features, I've often noticed those gaps show up later during exams or technical interviews where I actually need to know what I'm doing on the spot. Similar to the case scenario, the developer who over-relies on AI will eventually get put on the spot, such as during live code review or a system outage, and find that it  lacks the very expertise they were hired for.

Essentially, it all comes down to the intent behind using the tool, whether to supplement your own thinking or to replace it. This same logic applies to writing this very essay; if the question is asking about submitting work entirely generated by the tool, the answer would be no, as this is simply plagiarism. However, if a student writes their own original draft and then asks the AI to play devil's advocate and critique their argument and find flaws in their logic, then I believe the answer is yes. This process should amplify critical thinking as the student is still the author. Could an AI generate a 'better' essay than me in terms of grammar and structure? Probably, but better polished doesn't mean better content. The answer is no, because AI doesn't have my thoughts. It has patterns from millions of other essays, but it doesn't know what it's like to be me or my intention in writing something. We get out what we put in with these tools. It's possible for our ideas to be superseded by AI, just as they can be by the news, but this only happens to someone who is too reliant on it.

So, is it still worthwhile to have a career where AI can do all your work for you? My opinion is no. There are nuances in assuming that AI can do most of the work. As we've observed throughout history, each technological evolution results in job loss and economic displacement. However, there were always new roles that got created, and most of the time, the number of jobs created outnumbered the lost ones.

With this understanding, the focus should be on how to adjust ourselves to these new technologies as they will create new demands, both for large established corporations and for individual contributors. If people were truly aware that these AI tools can simply replace junior developers, they should rather focus on different responsibilities and concerns, such as managing these AI tools and infrastructure, which can yield more output and better results. Rather than fearing AI replacement, we should be positioning ourselves to work alongside these tools effectively, understanding their limitations and leveraging their strengths. The goal is not to be replaced by AI, but to become the human engineer who knows how to wield it with skill, integrity, and virtue.
