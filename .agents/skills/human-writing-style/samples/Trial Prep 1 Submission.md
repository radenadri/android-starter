### **Part 1: Ethical Theories**

Two primary ethical theories support the position that our client, Ware Fray, behaved more ethically than his opponent, Allmart.

* **Deontology (Duty-Based):** This theory posits that the morality of an action should be based on whether that action itself is right or wrong under a series of rules, rather than based on the consequences of the action. Ware Fray fulfilled his ethical duties by being consistently transparent and honest, where he clearly presented the fact that FreeMax was non-commercial, open-source, beta software provided "as is," as well as disclosed all known bugs. Given that, he had no duty, contractual or ethical, to provide free and on-demand support for a multinational corporation using his student project in a critical production environment. Conversely, Allmart failed in its ethical duty to perform its own due diligence by negligently deployed untested software for a core business function.

* **Rule Utilitarianism:** focuses on outcomes that produce the most good for the most number of people. For Ware Fray's case, his product was created as an open-source project intended to provide a  tool to the community for free, aiming to benefit users like Savana.com who utilized it responsibly. Allmart suffered as the direct result of its own negligence to test the software, which is a standard practice.

### **Part 2: Legal Elements of the Case**

To win, the plaintiff (Allmart) must prove every one of the following legal elements. Our defense will focus on demonstrating that they cannot meet this, particularly regarding the existence of a warranty.

**Legal Element 1: The Defendant, Ware Fray, was a “seller” of goods.**

* 1a: Fray qualifies as a "seller." This element requires Fray to be a person who sells or contracts to sell goods, as defined by the Code of Virginia.

* 1b: A "sale" for a "price" occurred. This element requires that a transaction occurred in which title (permission to use the software) passed from Fray to Allmart for a price.

* 1c: The scholarship constituted the "price." This element requires the Allmart scholarship to have been provided as direct consideration, or payment, for the use of FreeMax.

**Legal Element 2: An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose existed.**

* 2a: Fray had reason to know Allmart's particular purpose. This element requires that Fray had reason to know Allmart's specific purpose for the software such as real-time, global pricing system.

* 2b: Fray knew Allmart was relying on his skill and judgment. This element requires that Fray was aware that Allmart was depending on his expertise as a student to ensure the software was suitable for this critical purpose.

* 2c: Allmart actually and reasonably relied on Fray’s skill and judgment. This element requires Allmart's reliance on Fray's skill and judgment to be both actual and justifiable given the circumstances, including all of Fray’s disclaimers and warnings.

**Legal Element 3: The implied warranty of fitness was breached.**

* 3a: This requires to show how the software was unfit for Allmart’s specific purpose, production pricing system, due to the bug.

**Legal Element 4: The breach was the direct cause of the plaintiff’s damages.**

* 4a: Allmart suffered quantifiable damages. This element requires that Allmart experienced actual, quantifiable financial losses.

* 4b: The software bug was the proximate cause of the damages. This element requires a direct causal link (proximate cause) between the software's bug and Allmart's financial losses, as opposed to losses caused by their own failure to test, lack of internal safeguards, or other intervening factors.

### **Part 3: Witness Testimony**

**Legal Element 1: The Defendant, Ware Fray, was a “seller” of goods.**

Ware Fray (Defendant):

* (a) "No, never. I never expected to make any profit from it."

  * This directly contradicts the commercial nature of the transaction, showing Fray had no intent to act as a seller.

* (b) "The scholarship was something I applied for on my own. It was not tied to FreeMax in any agreement."

  * This can be used to defeat the argument that the scholarship was a "price" for goods as it establish the connection between the software and the scholarship

**Legal Element 2: An implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose existed.**

Ware Fray (Defendant):

* (a) "I told them it was beta software, that it was open-source, and that it was provided ‘as is.’ I also made sure that all known bugs were posted on my website for transparency."

  * This directly defeats the element of reliance as Fray explicitly demonstrated disclaimers which made it unreasonable for any company to rely on his judgment for a critical application without extensive independent testing.

* (b) "My understanding was that we would have casual contact. I had no expectation that they would use it for such a critical function without doing their own extensive work on it first."

  *  This helps defeat element 2a and 2b, as it shows Fray was not aware of the full scope of Allmart's intended use or the degree to which they were relying on him.

Rudy Kipling (CTO of Savana.com):

* (a) "The expectation is that the user assumes the risk. That’s why the software often includes disclaimers like the ‘as is’ clause in the FreeMax source code. The user is responsible for their own testing and implementation."

  *  As an expert witness, Kipling establishes an industry standard that makes Allmart’s alleged reliance on Fray appear unreasonable and negligent.

* (b) "Our entire approach was based on the assumption that if anything went wrong, 'we'd be able to fix it on our own.'"

  *  This shows how a responsible technology company uses open-source software, contrasting with Allmart’s approach and undermining their reliance on Fray.

**Legal Element 3: The implied warranty of fitness was breached.**

Ware Fray (Defendant):

* (a) "It was Allmart’s responsibility. I assumed a user would be able to fix any bugs they encountered, which is common with open-source projects."

  * The “fitness” in this context implies that users should be able to test and resolve bugs themselves when relying on open source projects. 

Rudy Kipling (CTO of Savana.com):

* (a) "Yes, absolutely. We have not had any problems with FreeMax in our production environment because we conduct extensive testing."

  *  This suggests that the software was fit for its purpose when used correctly. It implies the "breach" was not in the software itself but in Allmart's implementation process.

* (b) "Our engineers developed a fix that prevented any damaging effects. We saw this as a normal part of implementing open-source software."

  *  This shows that the bug was not a fatal flaw that made the software unfit, rather it is a manageable issue that a competent user could and should have handled.

**Legal Element 4: The breach was the direct cause of the plaintiff’s damages.**

Ware Fray (Defendant):

* (a) "Yes, I was clear that I had a busy schedule as a student and might not have time for maintenance or bug fixes."

  *  This helps to break the chain of causation. Allmart knew Fray was unavailable for support yet went live anyway, meaning their decision to proceed without a support plan was an intervening cause of their damages.

Rudy Kipling (CTO of Savana.com):

* (a) "No, we did not. We avoided any losses because we are a technology company with extensive testing procedures. I believe the damages stem from Allmart’s internal processes, not the software itself."

  *  This provides an alternative cause for the damages, Allmart’s own corporate negligence which directly refutes the claim that the software bug was the sole and proximate cause.

* (b) "He provided the software with disclaimers and couldn't have reasonably foreseen a company would use it without proper testing and safeguards."

  *  This argues that the damages were a direct consequence of Allmart's implementation, rather than the result of the bug given that all software contains unforeseeable bugs.

**Ethical Question: Which party acted more ethically in this situation?**

(1) **Ethical Theory 1:** Deontology (Fray acted ethically by fulfilling his duty to be honest and transparent, while Allmart failed in its duty to perform due diligence.)

* Ware Fray (Defendant):

  * (a) "I told them it was beta software, that it was open-source, and that it was provided ‘as is.’ I also made sure that all known bugs were posted on my website for transparency."

    *  This statement will support the deontological defense, proving Fray fulfilled his primary duty to be truthful about the product’s nature and risks.

  * (b) "I was clear that I had a busy schedule as a student and might not have time for maintenance or bug fixes."

    *  This shows Fray’s honesty in setting clear boundaries and managing expectations, clearly stated his duty not to mislead Allmart about his availability for support.

* Rudy Kipling (CTO of Savana.com):

  * (a) "I believe the damages stem from Allmart’s internal processes, not the software itself."

    * This argument that Allmart failed its duty to protect its systems and shareholders through standard practices.

(2) **Ethical Theory 2:** Rule Utilitarianism (Fray’s actions created more overall good by providing a valuable open-source tool than the harm caused to Allmart, which resulted from their own negligence.)

* Ware Fray (Defendant):

  * (a) "It’s an open-source software project I started as a fun learning exercise and to use as a portfolio piece to help me get a job."

    * This highlights the positive intent behind the software's creation which benefits society more than the negligents to one negligent company.

* Rudy Kipling (CTO of Savana.com):

  * (a) "We use it to customize our product pricing, which gives us a significant competitive advantage... Yes, absolutely. We have not had any problems with FreeMax in our production environment."

    *  This shows the software's utility and the "good" it created. A responsible company was able to use Fray's free tool successfully which can be interpreted as his work provided an overall positive impact.

### **Part 4: Case Summary**

Based on the available testimony, our case is strong on legal grounds but has a significant ethical vulnerability. Our likelihood of defeating the plaintiff’s claim for "breach of implied warranty" is very high. We have a very strong case to disprove that an implied warranty ever existed (Legal Element 2), as both our client's testimony and the testimony of Rudy Kipling confirm the software was provided with clear "as is" disclaimers and that the industry standard requires user testing, making Allmart's reliance unreasonable. Furthermore, Kipling's testimony that the damages due to Allmart's internal processes can be a strong argument against causation (Legal Element 4). Our case to disprove Fray was a "seller" (Legal Element 1\) is also solid, although it relies on proving the scholarship was fully independent. 

Our weakness is the perception of Fray accepting money from Savana.com to prevent Allmart from fixing the bug, which the plaintiff will likely frame this as a greedy and malicious act. Our strategy will be focused on dismantling the legal elements of the plaintiff's claim while proving the Savana deal was a business decision to protect the company's intellectual properties, rather than an unethical act against Allmart.
